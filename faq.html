Support on the Isle of Wight

What support is available on the Isle of Wight?

There is a lot of help on the island, and much of it is free.

  • Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) offers free, independent advice, help finding your way around services, and loans of practical equipment such as loud doorbells and TV listeners. They can visit you at home or see you at their hub in Newport. Phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk.
  • NHS audiology at St Mary's Hospital, Newport provides free hearing assessments, NHS digital hearing aids, batteries and repairs. Your GP can refer you. Phone 01983 552205. See the Isle of Wight NHS Trust Audiology Department (opens in a new tab).
  • Hearing Link Services, part of Hearing Dogs for Deaf People, runs support sessions for people with hearing loss, mostly online. See the Hearing Link Services outreach page (opens in a new tab) or ask Wight Sense.
  • Lipreading classes can make conversations much less tiring. Ask Wight Sense whether a class is running near you.
  • High street hearing centres offer private options and faster appointments: Specsavers Newport (opens in a new tab), Boots Hearingcare Newport (opens in a new tab) and the independent Isle of Wight Hearing Centre (Ryde).

Hearing tests and hearing aids

I've never been to audiology. What happens at an appointment?

It is natural to feel nervous, but the appointment is relaxed and painless. An adult appointment usually lasts between 30 minutes and an hour. There are three simple steps:

  • A friendly chat. The audiologist asks about your general health, your lifestyle and the situations where you find it hard to hear.
  • An ear check. They look inside your ears with a small handheld light called an otoscope, to check the health of your ear canal and see whether earwax is blocking the sound.
  • The hearing test. You wear comfortable headphones and listen to soft tones and beeps at different pitches. Each time you hear one, you press a button.

Afterwards they explain your results clearly. If a hearing aid or other support could help, they talk you through your options.

How to book: Ask your GP for a free referral to the Isle of Wight NHS Trust Audiology Department (opens in a new tab) at St Mary's Hospital in Newport. If you prefer a high street setting, you can book a hearing assessment at Specsavers Newport (opens in a new tab) or Boots Hearingcare Newport (opens in a new tab).

Is my hearing loss bad enough for hearing aids?

Many people wait until they are struggling badly, but even mild hearing loss can leave you tired from concentrating, make you miss jokes and shake your confidence. If your hearing is affecting your daily life, it is worth looking into.

  • Catching it early keeps your brain used to processing sound, which makes adapting to hearing aids easier later.
  • Reducing strain. Even a little help can stop you feeling exhausted after a simple conversation.
  • Clear information. A simple, painless test shows where your hearing is, so you can decide calmly and without pressure.

Where to get your hearing tested:

  • Your GP can check for earwax and refer you for a free test at St Mary's Hospital Audiology Department in Newport (01983 552205).
  • Wight Sense can talk through how your hearing is affecting you in a relaxed, non-medical setting: 01983 240222.
  • High street centres offer quick, free initial assessments and private options: Specsavers Newport (opens in a new tab) (01983 821280), Boots Hearingcare Newport (opens in a new tab) (0345 270 1600) and the Isle of Wight Hearing Centre (Ryde) (0800 542 2122).
My hearing seems to be getting worse. Should I be worried?

Try not to worry. Gradual changes in hearing are very common, and once you know what is happening they can usually be managed.

If your hearing drops suddenly, in one or both ears, over a few hours or days, or you also have severe dizziness, get urgent medical advice straight away from a doctor or NHS 111.

If the change is gradual, for example you are slowly turning the TV up or finding speech more muffled over several months, a routine appointment is the best next step:

  • Your GP surgery can look in your ears for simple, treatable problems such as earwax or a minor infection, and refer you to St Mary's Hospital Audiology Department (01983 552205).
  • Wight Sense is a gentle, non-medical place to talk things over first: 01983 240222.
  • High street providers can check whether your current hearing aids need adjusting, or whether you need a new prescription: Specsavers Newport (opens in a new tab) (01983 821280), Boots Hearingcare Newport (opens in a new tab) (0345 270 1600) and the Isle of Wight Hearing Centre (Ryde) (0800 542 2122).
I'm embarrassed to wear hearing aids.

Many people feel self-conscious at first, but modern hearing aids look very different from older ones.

  • Almost invisible. Many are tiny and sit inside the ear canal, or use ultra-thin clear tubes that hide behind the ear.
  • Like earbuds. Plenty of people wear wireless earbuds for music and calls, so hearing aids look entirely natural.
  • Staying connected. Wearing them is a positive step that helps you keep up with family, friends and conversation.

Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) can show you different types of modern hearing technology in a relaxed, non-medical setting, with no pressure (phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk). You can also find out about Hearing Link Services outreach page (opens in a new tab) sessions, where you can talk to people who understand how you feel.

I tried hearing aids before and they didn't help.

That is disheartening, but it does not mean nothing can help. Getting hearing aids to work well often takes fine-tuning, patience and the right support.

  • Technology has moved on. Modern digital hearing aids are much better at separating speech from background noise.
  • Fine-tuning matters. Your hearing can change over time, and a device that felt too loud or uncomfortable can often be reprogrammed.
  • Fit matters. If the piece in your ear did not fit properly it can cause whistling or discomfort. A different style or a custom fit can make a big difference.

Where to go:

  • St Mary's Hospital NHS Audiology Department can book you an adjustment or reassessment, tweak your current settings or upgrade you to a newer model: 01983 552205.
  • Wight Sense can suggest equipment such as extra loud doorbells or TV listeners to use alongside, or instead of, hearing aids: phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk.
  • Independent specialists, such as the Isle of Wight Hearing Centre (Ryde) (0800 542 2122), can let you try different technology. Ask about trial periods.

Everyday life with hearing loss

I struggle in noisy situations.

Struggling in busy cafes, family meals or community events is very common with hearing loss. You do not have to sit back and miss out.

  • Choose your seat. A spot with your back to a wall, away from the busiest part of the room, helps keep background noise down.
  • Use good lighting. Face the person speaking so you can see their expression and lips. This helps your brain fill in missing words.
  • Check your hearing aids. Modern ones can reduce background noise and boost speech.
  • Try assistive devices. A small wireless microphone clipped to a friend's clothing, or placed on the table, sends their voice straight to your ears or hearing aids.

Local help: Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) gives free practical support and can loan specialist equipment to island residents. You can self-refer by dropping into their hub, or by calling 01983 240222 or emailing enquiry@wightsense.org.uk. If you have NHS hearing aids, the audiology team at St Mary's Hospital can fine-tune them for noisy places or discuss wireless add-ons: 01983 552205.

My husband or wife has the TV on too loud.

This is one of the most common signs of hearing loss. Modern TV speakers often make speech sound muffled while background music stays loud. There are simple ways for you both to enjoy the TV together:

  • TV listening devices send the sound wirelessly to headphones or a neck loop worn by the person who struggles. They can set their own volume while the TV stays at a comfortable level for you.
  • Subtitles are free and take the strain out of fast dialogue and unfamiliar accents.
  • Soundbars with a speech or dialogue setting boost voices and reduce background effects.
  • Hearing aids with Bluetooth can link straight to the TV and send clear speech into the ears.

Try before you buy: Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) can demonstrate TV listeners and other assistive technology, and can arrange a home trial so your partner can test equipment before deciding (phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk). For Bluetooth hearing aids, you can ask the Isle of Wight Hearing Centre (Ryde) (0800 542 2122) or Specsavers Newport (opens in a new tab) (01983 821280).

I feel isolated because of my hearing.

Feeling left out of conversations is a real, tiring and emotional part of hearing loss. Your feelings are valid, and you are not alone. Here are some ways to reconnect:

  • Peer support. Talking with people who share your experience, over a cup of tea, can change everything.
  • Lipreading classes. They are sociable and friendly, and give you practical tools while you meet others in the same situation.
  • Family awareness. Small changes in how family members speak to you can make you feel included again. Our Communication tips page has some ideas.

Where to start:

  • Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) is a good first call. As well as practical help, they can put you in touch with local peer support and social groups: phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk.
  • Hearing Link Services, part of Hearing Dogs for Deaf People, runs informal support sessions. See the Hearing Link Services outreach page (opens in a new tab).
  • Adult education courses at the Isle of Wight College sometimes include lipreading and communication classes. Check what is currently on offer.
  • You are welcome to join our own WhatsApp Community, or find us on Facebook. Details are on our Get in touch page.

Tinnitus

What is tinnitus?

Tinnitus is hearing a sound in your ears or head that does not come from outside. It is very common, and closely linked to hearing loss. When your brain notices fewer sounds from the outside world, it can create its own background sound. For some people it is a high-pitched ringing, whistling or buzzing. For others it is a low hum, hissing or whooshing. It can come and go, or be constant.

It can feel overwhelming at first, but tinnitus is very rarely a sign of anything serious. If you are worried, see your GP. There is no single medicine that switches it off, but your brain can learn to ignore it over time. This is called habituation.

How tinnitus is managed

  • Hearing aids. Tinnitus is more noticeable in silence. Hearing aids bring back everyday sounds, which gives your brain something else to focus on.
  • Sound therapy. Soft background sound, such as a fan, gentle music or water sounds, can make tinnitus less noticeable, especially in a quiet bedroom.
  • Stress management. Feeling anxious, tired or stressed can make tinnitus seem louder. Relaxation techniques can reduce it.

Support on the island

  • St Mary's Hospital NHS Audiology Department runs a Tinnitus Management Service beside the ENT clinic in Newport, offering advice, sound generators and counselling. Ask your GP for a referral. Address: Audiology Department, St Mary's Hospital, Parkhurst Road, Newport, PO30 5TG. Phone 01983 552205.
  • Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) gives non-medical emotional support to anyone distressed by tinnitus, including advice on sleep. Millbrooke House, 137 Carisbrooke Road, Newport, PO30 1DD. Phone 01983 240222 or email enquiry@wightsense.org.uk.
  • Tinnitus UK (opens in a new tab) has a free helpline on 0808 808 6666, plus live web chat and online support groups.
  • Isle of Wight Tinnitus Support Group is a local, independent group for people living with tinnitus and with hyperacusis (sensitivity to everyday sounds). It offers a welcoming place to share experiences and hold regular meetings. Email iowtinnitus@gmail.com or visit tinnitus-iow.org (opens in a new tab).

Hearing dogs

Is it worth getting a hearing dog, and how do I apply?

Applying for a hearing dog is a personal decision, but for many people it brings comfort, independence and safety.

What a hearing dog offers

  • Practical safety. At home, a trained dog acts as your ears. It alerts you to sounds such as the doorbell, a cooking timer or an alarm clock by touching you with a paw or nose and leading you to the sound. For a smoke alarm, it is trained to lie down immediately to signal danger.
  • Companionship. A loyal companion can reduce anxiety and boost your confidence in public. The dog's uniform also tells other people you may have a hearing loss.

Things to think about. Having a dog is a big commitment. You need the time, physical ability and a secure home to look after its daily exercise and welfare. The charities fund the dog's training. Ask them which costs, if any, you would be responsible for.

How to apply on the Isle of Wight

  • Hearing Dogs for Deaf People (opens in a new tab) is a national charity that matches trained hearing dogs and Sound Support dogs with adults with severe to profound hearing loss. You can refer yourself online, or a family member can do it for you. They assess your lifestyle, home and communication needs to find the right match. They work with local partners such as Wight Sense. Check their website for who is eligible.
  • Ability Dogs 4 Young People (opens in a new tab) is a local island charity that trains assistance dogs for children and young people, to help them move on to independent schooling or work. Contact them to ask whether they can help a young person with hearing loss, and about age limits. Phone 01983 216246 or email info@abilitydogs4yp.org.uk.

Children, young people and families

  • Isle of Wight Council Specialist Teacher Advisory Service (hearing impaired) (opens in a new tab) is part of the council's SEN Service. Visiting Teachers of the Deaf support deaf learners at home, in nurseries, in mainstream and specialist schools, and in colleges across the island. Contact it through your child's school SENCo or the Council Contact Centre on 01983 821000 (Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm).
  • Children with Disabilities Service. Children and Family Services, County Hall, Newport, PO30 1UD. Phone 01983 823084 or 01983 823435 for an initial assessment, or email childrens.disability@iow.gov.uk.
  • Isle of Wight SENDIASS (opens in a new tab) gives free, independent information and advice about education, health and care (EHC) needs assessments for deaf children. Phone 01983 825548 or email sendiass@iow.gov.uk.
  • Wight Sense (opens in a new tab) supports people of all ages with hearing loss, sight loss or both. It offers assessments, home visits and practical help, including equipment such as flashing doorbells and vibrating alarms, and advice on looking after hearing aids. Open Monday to Friday, 9am to 4pm. Phone 01983 240222.
Is there support for parents and carers of deaf children?
  • Isle of Wight Deaf Children's Association is a local group run by parents and carers of deaf children, working with local health and education professionals. It provides equipment and support, and runs a Saturday Club with activities for deaf children and their families. It is linked to the National Deaf Children's Society (NDCS).   • National Deaf Children's Society (opens in a new tab) is the UK's leading childhood deafness charity, offering support, information and advice to deaf children and everyone who cares about them. It can also help you find a local group: localgroups@ndcs.org.uk.

BSL, Deaf culture and communication

  • Deaf Action (opens in a new tab) runs BSL courses from a taster course through Introduction to BSL to Levels 1, 2 and 3, all taught by qualified deaf tutors. Email learn@deafaction.org. Its Facebook page (opens in a new tab) lists course dates.
  • Isle of Wight Council Adult Community Learning offers BSL and Deaf Awareness courses as part of adult education. Search for Adult Community Learning on the council website (opens in a new tab).

  • Community support from specialist staff trained to support deaf, deafblind, deafened and hard of hearing people across the south of England, including the Isle of Wight.
  • Easthill Home for Deaf People (opens in a new tab) is a residential care home for BSL users at 7 Pitt Street, Ryde, PO33 3EB. Phone 01983 564068.
  • BSL courses. See the question above.
What is Deaf culture?

Deaf culture is the shared language, history, values and traditions of people who use sign language, such as British Sign Language (BSL), as their main language. Many Deaf people see being Deaf as a language and cultural identity, not only a medical condition.

A capital "D" (Deaf) is used for people who share this identity and language. A lowercase "d" (deaf) describes the physical condition of hearing loss. Not everyone with hearing loss is part of Deaf culture. A person with profound hearing loss who speaks and does not sign may not identify as culturally Deaf. A hearing person, such as the child of Deaf adults, can be part of the community if they sign as their main language.

Things you may notice

  • BSL is a full language with its own grammar and expressions. It is not English on the hands.
  • Visual living. Homes often use flashing doorbells, vibrating alarms and light-based alerts.
  • Strong community. Deaf schools, clubs and sports groups have long been at the heart of Deaf life.
  • Good manners are visual. Keep eye contact, as looking away can seem rude. To get someone's attention, give a gentle tap on the shoulder, wave in their line of sight, or tap a table or the floor so they feel the vibration.

The British Deaf Association has more: What is Deaf culture? (opens in a new tab)

What is Total Communication?

Total Communication is an approach that uses every available way of communicating, so that a person can understand and be understood. It treats all methods as equally valid and combines them to suit the individual, rather than relying on one method.

It grew up in deaf education in the 1960s, as a response to "oralism", the old practice of making deaf children speak and lipread and forbidding sign language.

What it can include

  • Language-based methods: speech, British Sign Language, fingerspelling and Sign Supported English.
  • Natural and non-verbal cues: gestures, facial expressions, body language, lipreading and residual hearing helped by hearing aids or cochlear implants.
  • Symbols and sensory tools: pictures, symbol cards, objects of reference, communication books, drawings, touch cues and movement.

Total Communication is now also widely used by speech and language therapists, specialist schools and care providers, to support people with a wide range of communication needs, including autistic people, people with profound and multiple learning disabilities, and people with speech impairments or acquired brain injuries.
